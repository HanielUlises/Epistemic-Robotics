The pallet is in one of two aisles, and nobody knows which

The RoboticsAcademy multi-robot Amazon warehouse exercise gives a fleet a floor of racks, a pallet and a dock, and asks for a task planner that assigns the jobs. It never asks how a robot comes to know where the pallet is: somebody already knows, and the planner is that somebody. Restore the uncertainty and no sequence of actions delivers the pallet at all: every sequence commits in advance to an aisle, and half of them are wrong. What is needed is a policy.

The same floor as the original exercise: AWS RoboMaker's small warehouse, at its own coordinates. Domain in epddl-workspace/robot-warehouse, execution in ros2_ws/src/warehouse_demo, both in Epistemic-Robotics. Full report (PDF)

The run

One robot, one mission, one branch point

Gazebo on the left, RViz on the right. The map RViz draws is the one slam_toolbox is building from the robot's own laser as it goes: white where it has measured free floor, teal where nobody has measured anything yet. The caption band is burned in from the run's own log (the actions ePlanSys dispatched, and the moment the epistemic state settled), so it cannot drift from what the robot did. The status in the corner is a live check_formula query against the epistemic state, and it reads UNKNOWN until the robot has looked.

The fleet

Two robots, then three, and the branch that was never filmed

The run above takes the easy leaf. r1 looks into bay 2, the pallet is there, and it lifts it. Nothing on screen distinguishes that from a scripted fetch, which is a fair complaint to make of it: the claim that the plan is a policy is in the caption and not in the picture.

So here is the same mission with the pallet in the other aisle. Nobody is told: the domain, the instance and the goal are unchanged, and the only difference in the world is one coordinate in the simulator.

Two things happen here that cannot happen on the other leaf. The first is the backtrack: the racks stop 0.15 m short of the east wall, which is nothing to a robot 0.21 m across, so there is no way through from one aisle to the next and a wrong guess costs three extra moves. The building charges for it.

The second is the announcement. With two robots the planner never spends one: pickup is public, so the fleet watching r1 lift the pallet out of a bay already learns which bay it was. Add a third robot who must also know, and on this branch that stops working — what r2 and r3 have to know is about bay 2, and watching a lift in bay 3 settles bay 3. An absence is not something you can watch. So r1 has to say so: 

[epistemic_perception_r1] 'bay2' is clear: inspect_r1_bay2 observed e-inspect-empty
[look_action_r1] (Kw r1 pallet-at_bay2) holds after 0.8s
[epistemic_bt] [(look_into r1 bay2)] observed e-inspect-empty
[announce_action_r1] radioing the fleet at bay3
[warehouse_mission] executing announce
...
[warehouse_mission] mission complete: the policy reached its goal

Note which way the epistemic line reads at that moment. It goes green on [Kw. r1] pallet-at_bay2 = TRUE the instant the bay is found empty, because Kw is knowing whether. Coming to know a thing is false is coming to know it.

The two-robot run, where one robot never moves

Worth watching for what does not happen. r2 waits at receiving for the whole run and issues no action at all — the log has zero lines for it — and the goal is still about r2's knowledge. It comes to know which bay the pallet was in by watching r1 lift it, without going anywhere. That is the cheapest way to learn something, and the planner finds it without being told.

What is actually running

Fifty-five nodes, and which of them is allowed to know things

One planning layer for the fleet, one set of action nodes per robot, and one navigation stack per robot in a namespace of its own. Where the line falls between them is not arrangement: it is the difference between what the fleet knows and what a robot has seen.

Three places the boundary is load-bearing

Each robot maps for itself. Every mapper writes to that robot's own /rN/map, and perception reads only its own robot's. This costs three SLAM instances and it is not an optimisation to collapse: sensing in this domain is semi-private, so a shared map would make everything one robot sees immediately known to all of them, and the demonstration would be quietly proving something the domain does not say. It is also, mundanely, a trap — slam_toolbox names its services relative to the node and its map absolutely, so three mappers in three namespaces all publish the same /map unless it is remapped by hand.

The action nodes are not namespaced. They cannot be: the executor's actions_hub is a relative name, so a node inside a robot's namespace would advertise its own hub and never hear the fleet's. They live in the global namespace and decline each other's work through specialized_arguments, which the executor matches positionally against the action's own arguments.

The building is shared; the map is not. Nav2 plans over /floorplan, the rasterised AWS world, served once for the fleet — a warehouse robot is given the building. What it is not given is a record of what it has looked at, and that is the per-robot /rN/map the µ-calculus planner and perception both reason over.

The domain

One precondition is the whole point

Six zones: shipping and receiving at the two ends of the west corridor, the service lane along the rack fronts, and the two candidate aisles opening off it. Driving, lifting and unloading are public ontic actions, since a warehouse fleet tracks where its own members are. Looking is semi-private sensing. And lifting carries a conjunct that is not about the warehouse at all: 

(:event e-pickup
  :parameters (?i - agent ?z - zone)
  :precondition
    (and (at-ag ?i ?z) (bay ?z) (pallet-at ?z) ([?i] (pallet-at ?z))
         (not (carrying ?i)))
  :effects (:and (carrying ?i) (not (pallet-at ?z))))

(pallet-at ?z) is a claim about the warehouse; ([?i] (pallet-at ?z)) is a claim about the robot. The first makes lifting possible, the second makes it permitted. Delete the second and one recovers exactly the classical domain: a robot that happens to be standing in the right aisle lifts a pallet it has no reason to believe is there, and a plan that never looks at anything is valid. The rejection is checked, not asserted: 

# plank validate -a go_r1_dock_south_lane go_r1_lane_bay2 pickup_r1_bay2
false (pickup_r1_bay2 is not applicable in
       'init (X) go_r1_dock_south_lane (X) go_r1_lane_bay2')

Looking is written as semi-private sensing, with one event per answer and a <Kw. ?i> conjunct in both preconditions: the robot that looks learns the answer, anyone else present sees it look without learning what it saw, and an inspection that settles nothing is not an action but a no-op with a cost. With one robot the distinction is invisible; modelling it as public would nevertheless be a lie, and one that stops being harmless the moment a second robot is added.

The two events are contradictory and neither is known to apply when the plan is built, which is what makes the solution branch: 

go_r1_dock_south_lane           [public-ontic]
go_r1_lane_bay2                 [public-ontic]
inspect_r1_bay2                 [semi-private-sensing]
  +-- e-inspect-empty   (not pallet-at_bay2)
      go_r1_bay2_lane, go_r1_lane_bay3, pickup_r1_bay3,
      go_r1_bay3_lane, go_r1_lane_dock_south,
      go_r1_dock_south_corridor, go_r1_corridor_dock_north,
      unload_r1_dock_north                      => goal reached
  +-- e-inspect-found   (pallet-at_bay2)
      pickup_r1_bay2, go_r1_bay2_lane, go_r1_lane_dock_south,
      go_r1_dock_south_corridor, go_r1_corridor_dock_north,
      unload_r1_dock_north                      => goal reached

The product update along that policy is reproduced independently of the planner, from the model plank exported and the events it ground, and checked at every node: each action must apply, and the goal must hold at every leaf. tools/show_plan.py exits non-zero if the two disagree.

The floor

A gap of 0.15 m decides the shape of the domain

The grid is not a drawing that resembles the exercise's warehouse. It is rasterised from the collision meshes Gazebo itself loads for that world (the shell, six rack rows, the tall shelf on the west wall and the clutter between them), sliced at the heights a ground robot can strike. The two maps AWS ships beside that world were not used: they are SLAM captures, sparse and missing most of the racks, and planning on them would make every result an artefact of whoever drove the robot that day.

The rack rows stop 0.15 m short of the east wall. To a planner that treats a robot as a point, that gap is a corridor running the length of the rack block and joining every aisle to every other, and routes down it are routes no TurtleBot could drive, since the robot is 0.21 m across. Growing the obstacles by the robot's 0.105 m radius closes it and leaves 0.7 m of each aisle. So in the zone graph an aisle is a pocket with one mouth, there is no edge from one bay to the next, and a wrong guess costs three moves. That is the building, not a simplification of it.

Crossing it

Unmeasured floor is not free floor

The robot starts at shipping with an empty occupancy grid. The service lane is not blocked, it is unmeasured, and the µ-calculus route planner will not route across cells nobody has measured. So early in a run the honest answer to “how do I get to the lane” is that there is no known way, and the log says exactly that: 

to lane: at (-3.50, -9.30), asked 'lane', 0 legs; 0 cells of lane measured so far
no route to lane yet; going to look from (-1.96, -9.28)
to lane: at (-1.76, -9.03), asked 'lane', 15 legs, and the zone is not on the map yet
to lane: at (0.36, -8.83), asked 'frontier', 24 legs; 285 cells of lane measured

Standing still is not a reply to that. When the destination comes back unreachable the drive picks a frontier: a cell that is known free, has room for the robot and the planner's inflation around it, and has unmeasured floor within sensor range. It routes there instead, and asks again. Three details are load-bearing, and each was at some point a robot going nowhere: the target must border the unknown, or the map stops growing; the score must reward travelling, or the robot creeps at the blockage; and a chosen frontier must be committed to, or the robot alternates between two of them and travels nowhere at all.

None of that decides anything epistemic. It is how the robot earns the map that the route planner, and then perception, are entitled to reason about. And nothing in the ROS package decides what was seen: look_into faces the bay, holds the robot there, and ends when the model reports that r1 knows. What put that knowledge in the model is plansys2_epistemic_perception, classifying the two bays over the grid SLAM built and reporting which of the two events fired. If the action node decided it, the demonstration would be a puppet show.

Scaling

What it costs to add a robot

The two-agent instance moves the epistemic conjunct to the robot that never leaves receiving; the three-agent instance asks it of two robots, neither of whom goes to look. The mission does not get harder: the plan is the same shape and r1 still does all the walking. The search does.

Roughly an order of magnitude per robot (×18, then ×11). Every agent is another relation over the worlds, and a formula about what somebody knows has to be checked against all of them.

The announcement the planner declines to make

The two-agent policy contains no announcement, although the domain offers report-pallet-at. It does not need one: pickup is public, so the fleet seeing r1 lift the pallet out of a bay is already enough for r2 to know which bay it was. Add a third robot who must also know, and the planner does spend an action on it, inserting report-pallet-at_r1_bay3 on the branch where r1 found bay 2 empty, because there the knowledge comes from an absence, and watching a lift in bay 3 settles bay 3, not bay 2.

That is not something the domain was written to demonstrate. It is what the search found.

Reproducing it

The domain, the floor, and the full system

# one, two and three agents; the two rejections; the scaling table
bash epddl-workspace/robot-warehouse/validate.sh

# the floor, and the six route-planning cases, offline and over ROS
colcon build --packages-select epistemic_msgs mu_path_planner warehouse_scenario
bash scenarios/warehouse/run_demo.sh
colcon test --packages-select warehouse_scenario     # 12 tests

# the full system in Gazebo
ros2 launch warehouse_demo warehouse_demo_launch.py

A bay is visible from the service lane thirty to sixty seconds before the robot reaches it, and the epistemic state will not accept an observation from a robot it does not yet believe is in the bay. Perception keeps re-offering the reading until the executor has applied the drive: applicability_retries, set to 240. The default of 40 grids is a few seconds, which fits a building of small rooms and not a warehouse aisle.
