metres by twenty-one with two aisles. This one asks what changes when the floor
is forty-two by sixty-three, holds a hundred and fifty-three obstacles and nine
people walking through it, and is driven by three robots rather than two.

execution layer does, and most of this report is an account of what.}

\section{Scope, and what this report is for}

The claim under examination is narrow and worth stating before anything else.
It is \emph{not} that epistemic planning scales: the policy solved here has
four nodes and would be solved as quickly over a floor plan drawn on a napkin.
It is that the execution layer between such a policy and a real fleet holds
together at a scale where the floor is no longer trivially traversable, and
that the properties the policy is chosen for --- in particular, that one agent
must \emph{not} come to know something --- remain checkable when it does.

Two things follow from that framing. First, the interesting content of this
report is the failure analysis of Section~\ref{sec:defects}, not the mission
trace of Section~\ref{sec:run}: the mission is three actions long and its
trace could be summarised in a sentence. Second, the negative results matter as
much as the positive ones, and Section~\ref{sec:notestablished} states plainly
what forty metres of warehouse does not establish.

\section{The floor}

\subsection{Why an existing floor, and which}

Open-RMF routes over a navigation graph and a building map, and its
demonstrations ship both. The largest maps in \code{rmf\_demos} are the airport
terminal, at $279 \times 60$\,m with 92 named waypoints, and a campus at
$319 \times 246$\,m; neither is a warehouse. There is no large warehouse map
published for Open-RMF at all.

The candidate warehouse assets on Gazebo Fuel were therefore measured directly,
from their own collision meshes rather than from their catalogue descriptions.
Table~\ref{tab:fuel} records the result, and the discrepancy between what the
descriptions promise and what the geometry provides is the substance of it.

\code{Depot} is the asset usually reached for --- some fifty thousand
downloads --- and is the trap of the three. Its detail is entirely visual.

The floor finally used is \code{dynamic\_logistics\_warehouse}, which is AWS
RoboMaker's small warehouse tiled and furnished by hand: nine floor slabs,
153 obstacles drawn from fourteen AWS assets, five lamps, and nine scripted
actors walking fixed circuits. Measured from its own model poses it spans
$42.0 \times 62.8$\,m, against $14.05 \times 21.05$\,m for the floor of the
companion report --- an increase of roughly nine times in area.

\subsection{Deriving the roadmap}

The companion report's floor is parametric and its navigation graph is written
from a formula. This one is not: nothing about the placement of 153 hand-set
obstacles is regular, so the graph is derived from the world instead.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Every model in the world is one of fourteen AWS assets. Their collision
        extents are measured once, from the collision meshes, and cached.
  \item Three of the fourteen are structure rather than obstacle. Getting this
        wrong is not a detail: \code{GroundB} is a $14 \times 21$\,m floor tile,
        and treating it as an obstacle fills the warehouse solid.
  \item The remaining models are rasterised at 0.25\,m and inflated by the
        robot's radius plus a margin, giving 0.45\,m of clearance.
  \item A lattice is laid over the free floor, lanes are kept only where the
        straight segment between their ends is clear, and the roadmap is then
        thinned.
\end{enumerate}

The thinning is where the engineering is. A node is removed whenever every pair
of its neighbours can be joined directly, which preserves every route and costs
one waypoint; junctions and aisle waypoints are kept. Table~\ref{tab:lattice}
shows why both halves of that --- fine lattice, then thinning --- are
necessary.

Six of the racks are eighteen metres long, so the floor is crossed by walls
with few gaps. At two-metre spacing the lattice misses enough of those gaps to
leave the warehouse in three disconnected north--south bands, and a
largest-component filter then silently discards two thirds of the building. At
1.25\,m every gap is found. The thinning recovers what the fine spacing costs:
261 waypoints, 35 of them named as aisles, and 1652 directed lanes.

\subsection{Aisles are found, not listed}

A waypoint is called an aisle when the floor runs out close on both sides along
one axis and is open along the other. That is exactly the place a robot must
enter before it can tell anything about what is inside, and so exactly what a
sensing action wants to name. Thirty-five such waypoints survive a four-metre
minimum separation, one per aisle rather than one per cell.

\section{The fleet}

\subsection{A TurtleBot3 that Open-RMF can drive}

The fleet is three TurtleBot3 Waffles. Assembling one that RMF can actually
drive took two corrections, both of which are worth recording because neither
is documented anywhere obvious.

\paragraph{The skeleton} RMF moves a simulated robot with the \code{slotcar}
plugin, which is a kinematic driver rather than a controller. A Waffle built
from scratch to what slotcar documents it requires --- a \code{base\_footprint},
a \code{left\_wheel} and \code{right\_wheel}, and the revolute joints
\code{joint\_tire\_left} and \code{joint\_tire\_right} --- loaded without
complaint, initialised slotcar, published its state at 6\,Hz, accepted paths
from the fleet adapter, and did not move a centimetre. On the same floor and
the same graph, \code{rmf\_demos}' TinyRobot drove sixty metres without
trouble, which is what isolated the model as the fault rather than the world or
the roadmap. The Waffle geometry is therefore mounted on TinyRobot's drive
skeleton unchanged.

\paragraph{The scale} \code{turtlebot3\_gazebo} ships its meshes as STL in
millimetres. Loaded at scale 1 a Waffle base is 271 metres across. The
\code{.dae} files beside them in \code{turtlebot3\_common} are placeholder
cubes two metres on a side, which is a second and independent way of producing
an enormous robot. Both were encountered.

\subsection{Pinned allocation}

Tasks are pinned to named robots through \code{robot\_task\_request} rather
than dispatched for bidding. The reason is epistemic rather than practical: the
planner assigns actions to named agents while it solves, the model records
knowledge against those names, and if the dispatcher were free to substitute a
robot the model would credit an agent with having sensed something it never
saw. An agent bound to no robot is a hard error and the bridge refuses the
action.

\section{The mission}
\label{sec:run}

The domain is the survey of \code{eplansys}, unchanged. Three agents; a site
that may be contaminated; a goal of three conjuncts:
\[
\gamma \;=\; \Kw{\mathit{scout}}\,\mathit{contaminated}
\;\wedge\; \Kw{\mathit{relay}}\,\mathit{contaminated}
\;\wedge\; \neg\,\Kw{\mathit{observer}}\,\mathit{contaminated}.
\]

The third conjunct is what gives the domain its difficulty. It forbids the open
channel, which is otherwise the cheapest way for the team to inform itself, and
so forces the planner to choose a channel by its audience. The planner returns
a policy of four nodes, branching.

What the larger floor changes is the geography the policy is executed over. The
site is \code{aisle\_20}, thirty metres from the robot's charger and reachable
only by way of the service floor: an aisle cannot be seen into until a robot
stands at its mouth, which is the physical counterpart of the sensing action
the policy contains.

\section{Defects found by the integration}
\label{sec:defects}

Putting the chain in operation at this scale revealed eight defects that no
part of it exhibited alone. They are given in full because, taken together,
they are the substantive result of the exercise: six of the eight are
consequences of composition rather than errors of programming, and none would
have appeared in a unit test.

Three of these deserve comment.

\paragraph{The pallet jack} The graph check was written to refuse lanes that
cross obstacles, and it did refuse three layouts that looked correct on paper.
It passed this one because its obstacle set was racks and pillars: the prop had
been placed in the world generator, for appearance, and the checker never saw
it. The symptom was severe out of all proportion to the cause --- a robot
pressed motionless into a pallet jack while RMF reported its task underway and
nothing anywhere reported a collision. The correction moves the props into the
same definition the checker reads, and the check that missed it now rejects the
original placement.

\paragraph{The seam} A twelve-centimetre gap between two floor tiles is
invisible in any rendering and fatal to a rasteriser, which cannot distinguish
a seam from a hole. What makes it worth recording is the interaction with the
largest-component filter: the filter is correct in isolation --- an isolated
pocket of floor is a waypoint RMF will accept and never route to --- and
together they discard two thirds of a warehouse without a diagnostic.

\paragraph{The lattice} Connectivity and tractability pull in opposite
directions, and the resolution that satisfies one fails the other. Building
finely and thinning afterwards is not the same as building coarsely, and
Table~\ref{tab:lattice} is the measurement that says so.

\section{What forty metres does not establish}
\label{sec:notestablished}

\paragraph{Not a planning result} The policy has four nodes. Nothing here bears
on the complexity of epistemic planning, and the floor could be enlarged
indefinitely without touching it.

\paragraph{Not a study of contention} Three robots share one floor and three
tasks were accepted and executed concurrently without a process failure, but no
measurement was made of how the traffic schedule mediates the shared corridors
under sustained multi-robot traffic. That is the obvious next experiment and it
has not been run.

\paragraph{Not a dynamic-obstacle result} The world's nine actors walk fixed
circuits and are not in the navigation graph. The fleet does not avoid them and
was never asked to; they are scenery, and a robot that met one would drive into
it.

\paragraph{Not confidentiality} The private channel is realised by addressing:
an utterance is published to a topic whose subscribers are the audience the
action declares. Any node may subscribe to any topic. What is established is
that the team used a channel the excluded agent was not addressed on, not that
it could not have listened.

\section{Conclusion}

The execution layer holds at this scale, and the eight defects of
Section~\ref{sec:defects} are the price of finding that out. Six of them are
properties of the composition rather than of any component, which is the
recurring lesson of this line of work: a system of autonomous nodes, plugins
loaded at run time and streams advancing at different rates exhibits a family of
faults that integration alone can reveal.

The epistemic content is unchanged from the small floor, and deliberately so.
What the larger floor buys is that the geography is no longer trivial --- the
site is thirty metres away and down an aisle --- so that the claim the domain
exists to support, that an agent can be kept from coming to know something, is
made against a floor where getting anywhere at all is work.
