Link grades

Two robots start at opposite ends of a hall. A corridor along its north side may be blocked by debris, and only a robot standing at the mouth of the corridor can tell with its laser. Both robots must come to know whether the corridor is blocked. Each builds its own occupancy map from its own laser, and what one map holds reaches the other robot only over a link that a monitor can take down: never, for a window of the mission, or for all of it. These are the three link grades.

The goal is \(\mathit{Kw}_{r1}\,\mathit{blocked} \wedge \mathit{Kw}_{r2}\,\mathit{blocked}\). Five formulations are compared on the same seeds: two epistemic ones, planned by Aletheia over one EPDDL domain, and three classical ones, planned by POPF. They differ in what they assume about the link and in where that assumption is kept: in the model, in a reading taken before planning, in a timeout of the executor, or nowhere.

The epistemic policy with the link unknown met the goal on the maps in 60 of 60 runs, in every grade, in 62.7 s on average. The classical report, which records the receiver as informed once the message is sent, was reported successful in all its runs and backed by the receiver's map in 20 of 60. Reading the link before planning, epistemic or classical, finished in 30.5 s when the link held and in 123.2 s when it fell during the message. Judged by the maps, not by the executor: a run counts only if, at its end, each robot's own map decides the corridor, and decides it correctly. 20 seeds, each run under all five formulations and all three grades from the same start: 300 runs.

Package link_grades_demo; policies from eplansys, search by Aletheia, classical plans by POPF[4] through PlanSys2[5]; the link is cut by the comm_monitor and link_gate of A link that actually falls. Full account in Link Grades (PDF): the domain, the argument about worst-case planning and lossy messages, the classical formulations, the batch and the implementation defects.

Environment

Environment

The hall is 14 by 8 m. Along its north side runs a corridor 5 m long and 1 m wide, a dead end whose mouth opens west. In half the seeds a box of debris stands in its middle. From anywhere but the mouth the corridor's walls hide it, and a ray down the corridor ends on its end wall, so that a corridor without debris is seen to be empty and not merely not seen. r1 starts at the west dock, about 6 m of route from the mouth; r2 at the east station, about 13 m. Each robot has a spot at the mouth, side by side, so that neither stands in the other's view.

Each robot integrates its own scans into an occupancy grid of 5 cm cells over the whole hall. The grid decides the corridor when its cells in the outlined region say so: blocked when at least 6 of them are known occupied, clear when none is and at least 90 % of its 10 cm blocks hold a known free cell; otherwise it does not decide, which is the state of a robot that has not looked. Over the link each robot also receives the other's grid and merges it under its own: its own cell where it has one, the other's where it has none. What a robot knows about the corridor is read off this merged map.

The link is the one of A link that actually falls: a comm_monitor declares it up or down, and a link_gate on each side passes or drops every message between the robots accordingly, grids, decisions, reports and acknowledgements alike, in both directions at once. The monitor follows a fixed schedule in simulated time, so that no instant depends on where the robots are. Under the full grade the link is never down. Under the partial grade it falls 10 s into the mission and returns 24 to 100 s later, the outage growing by 4 s with each seed. Under the none grade it is down before the mission starts and never returns.

The domain

Actions, observers and goal

The actions are event models of dynamic epistemic logic[1], planned over as in[2]. The share has three designated events: delivered with the value blocked, delivered with the value clear, and lost. The first two require the link to be up and the third requires it to be down. Both robots observe which event occurred, the receiver because the message arrives or does not, the sender because the acknowledgement, carried by the same link, does or does not; the action type is a library of its own, link.epddl. The goal asks that each robot know whether the corridor is blocked; nothing in it mentions the link.

Two initial models are used. In the first, the link's state is unknown to both robots: the model has worlds in which it is up and worlds in which it is down, and neither robot can tell them apart. In the second, the mission reads the link from the monitor before planning, and the model makes its state common knowledge, up or down. A third model, with r1 at the mouth knowing the answer, r2 not knowing it and the link down, is the one the second formulation replans in when a share goes unacknowledged.

Worst-case planning

Why the policy never counts on the link

With the link unknown, no policy that tries the link is optimal under the worst case. Aletheia's AO*[3] returns a policy of least depth in its worst branch. A policy that shares has a branch in which the share is lost, and in that branch the lost event changes nothing that the fallback needs: r2 must still go to the mouth and look. That branch is therefore one step deeper than the fallback alone, and the fallback alone is a policy. Without action costs or likelihoods on the events, a planner of this kind never plans to try the link and fall back. On the task with the link unknown, Aletheia returns, at depth 4 after 23 expansions: 

goto-junction(r1) · inspect(r1)
  on e-inspect-blocked or e-inspect-clear: goto-junction(r2) · inspect(r2)

Both robots go and look; the executor dispatches the two drives together. This is the formulation called epistemic, link unknown below. Its policy coincides with the classical plan in which both robots visit, and it is reached by the planner from a model that represents the link and the robots' ignorance of it, without the modeller removing the link from the problem.

With the link's state given as common knowledge, the same domain yields a different policy. Up, Aletheia returns, at depth 3: 

goto-junction(r1) · inspect(r1)
  on e-inspect-blocked or e-inspect-clear: share(r1, r2)

No lost branch appears, because with the link known to be up the lost event is not applicable. When the link falls after the reading, the share is not acknowledged within 30 s; the executor reports the action failed, and the mission loads the third model and replans, which sends r2 to look. This is the formulation called epistemic, link read. Its recovery lies outside the policy, in a timeout and a replanning step, and it coincides by construction with the classical report and replan described next.

Classical planning

Three classical formulations

The classical domain has the same drives and inspections, a fact (knows ?r) for each robot and a fact (link); a report from r1 to r2 requires (link) and makes (knows r2) true. The goal is that both robots know. Three formulations differ in how the link enters the problem. In the first, visit, the problem omits (link), so POPF never relies on it and sends both robots to look. In the second, report, the problem always includes (link) and the report is sent without waiting for anything: once it leaves, the plan holds r2 informed. In the third, report and replan, the problem includes (link) only when the monitor says the link is up; the report then waits up to 30 s for an acknowledgement and fails without one, and the mission removes (link) from the problem expert's state and replans, so that POPF sends r2. The third is the strongest of the three and the one a careful engineer would deploy; its wait and the epistemic share's are the same parameter, read from the same code.

None of the three can state the goal as knowledge. The fact (knows r2) holds in the plan as soon as a report that requires a link is applied, and only the acknowledgement logic of the third formulation, which is outside the classical model, keeps it from being asserted when the message was lost.

Measured on the maps

Reported success and success backed by the maps

A run is reported when the executor ends the mission with success. It is backed when, in addition, at the end of the mission each robot's merged map decides the corridor and decides it correctly, and unbacked when it is reported and not backed: the system holds that both robots know and a robot's map does not show it. Mission time is measured in simulated seconds from a common start. The time at which the later of the two maps first decided the corridor correctly is recorded separately, whether or not the mission had ended, together with the distance each robot drove and the area of the hall its map holds. Coverage enters the evaluation through the region that decides the goal; the known area is reported beside it, since a robot that drives further maps more of the hall without knowing more about the corridor.

Implementation

Implementation defects

Two defects were found while the experiment was built. The first is the one recorded on the page of the watcher: ePlanSys's executor checks the modal conjuncts of all of an action's designated events together, as a single conjunction, so that a share whose delivered events required \(K_{r1}\,\mathit{blocked}\) in one and \(K_{r1}\,\neg\mathit{blocked}\) in the other could never be dispatched. Each such event now requires \(\mathit{Kw}_{r1}\,\mathit{blocked}\) together with its own fact, which is equivalent. The second concerned the classical report and replan: the mission removed (link) from the problem expert's state by matching its text, which the problem expert prints with inner spaces, so the replan kept the link and tried the report again. The match was corrected, and every run on this page was made after the correction. One run, the unknown link under the partial grade on seed 4, was discarded and run again: its simulator's clock never advanced past the start, and the run reached the wall-time limit with both drives dispatched and neither robot moved. It is kept apart in the artifacts.

Limits

Limitations

The results hold under several restrictions of the model. The link drops both directions together, so that a message cannot arrive while its acknowledgement is lost; with independent directions the receiver could know while the sender does not know that it knows, and the share would need a fourth event or a weaker observability for the sender. Aletheia's AO* minimises the depth of the worst branch and has no costs or likelihoods: under that criterion trying the link never pays, and a policy that tries the link and falls back would require a planner that weighs the delivered branch against the lost one, by expected cost or by a bound on the probability of loss. Neither the domain nor the planner was altered to obtain such a policy.

The experiment has restrictions of its own. The two epistemic formulations coincide by construction with two classical ones, the unknown link with the visit and the read link with the report and replan, so that the comparison on time measures the assumptions about the link and not the planners. The partial grade cuts the link at a fixed instant for an outage whose length grows with the seed; in the 7 seeds whose outage ends within the acknowledgement's wait, a read link is acknowledged late and no replanning occurs. Mission time includes the planners' own wall time, which ran while the simulation advanced.
