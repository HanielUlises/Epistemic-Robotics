The Epistemic Robotics Project

Long-term Research Program

Robots that reason about
what they know,
and what they do not.

Epistemic robotics is the study of autonomous systems that maintain formal representations of knowledge, belief, and uncertainty — not only about the physical world, but about the epistemic states of other agents operating within it. This project develops planning formalisms, runtime architectures, and geometric reasoning tools grounded in Dynamic Epistemic Logic and the µ-calculus, targeting heterogeneous multi-robot fleets under partial observability and communication constraints.

The Problem

Classical planning assumes a world fully known

A robot navigating a warehouse, a drone team conducting search-and-rescue, a fleet of heterogeneous agents coordinating through communication blackouts — all share a challenge that classical task planning cannot address. The world is not fully observable, and the agents within it hold genuinely different, potentially inconsistent views of what is true.

Planning under partial observability requires more than probabilistic state estimation. It requires a robot to represent not only what it believes about the world, but what it believes other agents believe — and to plan actions that deliberately shape the epistemic state of the system as a whole. This is the problem of epistemic planning, and it demands a logic capable of reasoning about knowledge as a first-class object.

The following goals are not expressible in any classical planning formalism. They are epistemic goals — and they arise routinely in the deployment of autonomous multi-robot systems.

"Robot R₁ must know whether the corridor ahead is blocked."
\(\mathrm{Kw}_{R_1}(\textit{blocked}(c_1))\)
"All robots in the fleet must share common knowledge that the mission is complete."
\(C_{\mathcal{A}}\,(\textit{mission\_complete})\)
"Robot R₂ does not yet know the current location of R₁."
\(\neg\, K_{R_2}\bigl(\textit{at}(R_1, \ell)\bigr) \;\text{ for all } \ell\)
"R₁ knows that R₂ believes the package has been delivered."
\(K_{R_1}\, K_{R_2}\,(\textit{delivered}(p))\)
"Every robot knows whether its assigned task has been completed — but not necessarily the tasks of others."
\(\bigwedge_{i \in \mathcal{A}}\, \mathrm{Kw}_i\bigl(\textit{done}(\tau_i)\bigr)\)

None of these goals have a representation in STRIPS, PDDL, or any classical planning formalism. They require an epistemic planner — a system whose state space is not a set of ground atoms, but a structured model of what every agent knows, believes, and is uncertain about. The gap between classical planning and epistemic planning is not a matter of expressivity at the margins; it is a categorical difference in what kind of object constitutes a world state.

Formal Framework

Dynamic Epistemic Logic and the product update

The project is grounded in Dynamic Epistemic Logic (DEL), a modal framework for reasoning about how informational actions — sensing, announcement, communication — transform the knowledge states of agents. The central semantic object is the Kripke model: a labeled graph whose nodes are possible worlds and whose edges, indexed by agent, encode what each agent considers epistemically indistinguishable from its current vantage point.

\[ M = (W,\, R,\, L), \quad W \neq \emptyset,\quad R : \mathrm{Ag} \to 2^{W \times W} \] \[ M, w \models \Box_i \varphi \;\iff\; \forall v : w\, R_i\, v \;\Rightarrow\; M, v \models \varphi \] \[ \mathrm{Kw}_i\,\varphi \;\;\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\;\; \Box_i \varphi \;\vee\; \Box_i \neg\varphi \qquad\qquad C_G\,\varphi \;=\; \bigwedge_{k > 0} \Box_G^k\,\varphi \]

The product update \(s \otimes a\) is the core operation. Given an epistemic state and an event model describing an action's physical and informational effects simultaneously, it computes the resulting epistemic state. Physical ontic effects, private sensing, and public communication are unified under this single operator.

\[ W' = \{\,(w, e) \in W \times E \;\mid\; M, w \models \mathrm{pre}(e)\,\} \] \[ R'_i = \{\,((w,e),(v,f)) \;\mid\; w\, R_i\, v \;\wedge\; e\, Q_i\, f\,\} \] \[ L'(w,e) = \{\,p \in P \;\mid\; M, w \models \mathrm{post}(e)(p)\,\} \]

Two structural properties bound the computational cost of runtime planning. An event model \(\mathcal{E}\) is self-absorbing if \(M \otimes \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{E} \simeq M \otimes \mathcal{E}\) for all \(M\) — applying it twice has the same effect as once. Two event models \(\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{E}'\) are commutative if \(M \otimes \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{E}' \simeq M \otimes \mathcal{E}' \otimes \mathcal{E}\) for all \(M\). When an event library satisfies these conditions, the Kripke model remains bounded in size under any sequence of updates, and the bisimulation contraction can be computed in linear time. This tractability guarantee is a design constraint that propagates through every action schema in the system: it is the formal bridge between the expressivity demands of DEL planning and the latency constraints of physical deployment.

Public announcement

All agents simultaneously learn \(\varphi\). Accessibility relations collapse for \(\varphi\) across the group, establishing \(C_G\,\varphi\) in a single update.

Private sensing

The acting agent resolves its uncertainty about \(\varphi\). Others may observe that an action occurred without learning its outcome — a fundamental asymmetry in distributed systems.

Ontic action

Modifies the physical world via postconditions. Uninformed agents retain prior beliefs; the actor and any witnesses update via the product update accordingly.

Higher-order belief

Nested modalities \(\Box_i \Box_j \varphi\) encode what agent \(i\) believes agent \(j\) knows. Theory of Mind arises naturally from the Kripke semantics without additional machinery.

The challenge of multi-agent coordination under communication restrictions makes the depth of this reasoning non-trivially consequential. Two robots approaching the same task simultaneously can resolve the assignment ambiguity without any explicit communication, if each reasons to sufficient depth about the other's observations. Third-order reasoning — \(\Box_i \Box_j \Box_k \varphi\) — reduces posterior variance on joint task-assignment configurations substantially over first-order baselines. Robots can signal intent through motion alone by selecting policies that minimize expected free energy over the higher-order belief structure, an approach grounded in variational active inference and formalized directly through DEL product update semantics over the empathy-state representation.

Whether a particle-based approximation of the Kripke model suffices for robust multi-robot coordination — or whether exact product update semantics are necessary in adversarial conditions — is one of the long-horizon questions this project is built to answer. The adversarial DEL planning problem, in which the planner must find a partial strategy \(\hat{\sigma}\) achieving the epistemic goal \(\varphi_g\) under all legal completions by an opponent or nondeterministic environment, provides the game-theoretic framework for the hardest variant of this question.

Geometric Reasoning

The µ-calculus over occupancy grids

A robot under SLAM maintains an occupancy grid: a discrete probabilistic map in which each cell is classified as free, occupied, or unknown. The project treats this structure as a Kripke model for spatial reasoning, where cells are possible worlds and the edge relation encodes navigable adjacency. The µ-calculus — a modal fixed-point logic extending propositional dynamic logic with least and greatest fixed-point operators — evaluates epistemic properties directly over this graph.

\[ \Gamma : \mathrm{OGM} \;\longrightarrow\; K_{\mathrm{nav}} \qquad K_{\mathrm{nav}} = \bigl(C,\; E_{\mathrm{adj}},\; L_{\mathrm{sem}}\bigr) \]

The function \(\Gamma\) lifts the occupancy grid to a Kripke navigation model. Each SLAM update applies an event model \(E_{\mathrm{exp}}\) via product update. The central empirical question of the project's experimental program is whether \(\Gamma\) commutes with this update:

\[ \Gamma\!\left(\mathrm{OGM}^{(t+1)}\right) \;\cong\; K_{\mathrm{nav}}^{(t)} \otimes E_{\mathrm{exp}} \]

This commutativity condition — that the geometric and epistemic updates are order-independent — is not trivially satisfied. It depends on the consistency between the navigation accessibility relation \(R_{\mathrm{nav}}\) and the semantic labeling produced by the certification layer. Its failure would mean that incremental epistemic reasoning over SLAM graphs is unsound, and would require full recomputation of the Kripke model at every map update. Its truth, conversely, licenses a modular architecture in which geometric and epistemic updates are decoupled.

Three core formulas drive the planning interface. Each is a fixed-point computation evaluated incrementally as the SLAM graph grows. The figures below render each formula over a 14×12 occupancy grid. Exploration frontier — maximum epistemic value

\(\varphi_{\mathrm{expl}}\) has no fixed-point: it is evaluated pointwise. A cell satisfies it iff none of its navigable neighbors belongs to the unknown region — constituting a locally complete S5 knowledge block. The frontier (amber) is the set of cells where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{expl}}\) fails: this is the zone of maximum epistemic value for the planner, where observation actions yield the greatest reduction in world count. The exploration problem is therefore cast as a planning problem whose goal is \(\forall v \in C : v \models \varphi_{\mathrm{expl}}\) — a purely epistemic goal over the geometry of the environment.

The epistemic overhead of these computations is bounded: the certification cost for \(\varphi_p\) is \(O(k \cdot r^2_{\mathrm{eval}} / \delta^2 \cdot |\varphi|)\), local and independent of the global graph size \(|C^{(t)}|\). Whether the latency \(\lambda_\Gamma = \lambda_{\mathrm{nav}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{cert}}\) satisfies \(\mathrm{P}_{95}(\lambda_\Gamma) < 100\,\mathrm{ms}\) in nominal SLAM conditions is a core falsifiable hypothesis of the experimental program.

Multi-Agent Coordination

Heterogeneous fleets under communication restrictions

The hardest problems arise when multiple robots must coordinate while operating in communication-restricted environments. Each robot maintains a local Kripke model of the system's joint epistemic state. When robots are connected, public announcements collapse accessibility relations and establish common knowledge. When they are disconnected, each robot evolves its model independently through DEL product updates over local observations alone — producing genuinely heterogeneous epistemic states that cannot be resolved by a central world model.

A particle-based approximation of the Kripke model enables runtime use. Each robot \(i\) maintains a finite set of belief particles — including second-order particles representing what it believes other robots believe — and propagates them forward using known dynamics and the product update operator. The common belief set \(C_i \subseteq \mathcal{P}_i\) corresponds to the last globally shared epistemic state. When communication resumes, a gossip protocol routes task assignments through predicted teammate locations, exploiting the particle model to minimize rendezvous cost.

\[ p_{ij,k} \;=\; \text{robot } i\text{'s belief about robot } j\text{'s belief about robot } k\text{'s state} \] \[ s^i_{t-1} \otimes \mathrm{announce}(\Omega) \;\models\; K_i \sigma^i_t \;\wedge\; \bigwedge_{j \in \mathcal{A}} K_i K_j \sigma^j_t \]

The epistemic task allocation problem is cast as an integer program over the belief state, whose solution produces a policy as a concatenation of gossip bundles and task bundles. The allocation problem is NP-hard in general, but the particle-based belief approximation renders it tractable via a genetic algorithm with a greedy feasible-solution seed.

The deeper challenge is implicit coordination: resolving task assignment ambiguity without any communication, by reasoning about what other robots observe. The active epistemic inference formulation casts this as minimization of variational free energy over the higher-order belief structure:

\[ \mathcal{F}(x_i, u_i) \;=\; H\!\left[q(\mathcal{G})\right] + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(\mathcal{G})\;\|\; P(x_i \mid y_i, \omega_i)\right] \]

Policies that actively disambiguate goal assignments by taking movements informative to observers minimize the epistemic term of the expected free energy. Robots signal intent through motion without explicit communication, resolving assignment conflicts that would stalemate first-order reasoners. The advantage grows markedly as team size exceeds three robots, and only materializes at third order — the depth at which a robot models what a teammate believes it itself observes.

Planning System

Epistemic planning tasks and EPDDL

An epistemic planning task is a triple \((s_0, \mathrm{Act}, \varphi_g)\): an initial epistemic state, a finite set of epistemic actions, and a goal formula drawn from the full epistemic language. A solution is a sequence — or, for nondeterministic domains, a conditional plan — transforming \(s_0\) into a state satisfying \(\varphi_g\).

EPDDL, the Epistemic Planning Domain Definition Language introduced for the IPC 2026 Epistemic Planning Track, provides a standardized syntax for expressing these tasks. It grounds DEL semantics in a PDDL-compatible format through an action type library system that decouples the structural observability pattern of an action from its specific preconditions and effects. Planning problems separate into two distinct syntactic layers:

\[ \text{Epistemic goal:}\quad \mathrm{Kw}_{\mathrm{All}}\bigl(\textit{blocked}(c_1)\bigr) \;\wedge\; K_{R_1}\bigl(\textit{at}(p,\, \textit{storage})\bigr) \;\wedge\; C_{\{R_1,R_2\}}\bigl(\textit{done}(\alpha)\bigr) \]
;; EPDDL domain fragment — sensing and announcement actions (:action sense-corridor :type sensing :actor ?r - robot :parameters (?r - robot ?c - corridor) :observability ((full-obs ?r) (no-obs All \ {?r})) :precondition (adjacent ?r ?c) :sense-formula (blocked ?c)) (:action announce-blocked :type public-announcement :actor ?r - robot :precondition (and (adjacent ?r ?c) ([?r] (blocked ?c))) :announced-formula (blocked ?c))

The separation is intentional. The goal formula lives in the epistemic language and is rendered mathematically; the action schema lives in the planning language and is rendered as code. Conflating the two syntactically obscures the theoretical architecture that makes EPDDL expressive: the goal is a modal formula over a Kripke model, while the action schema is a template for constructing event models whose product with the current state produces a new epistemic state. The planner operates entirely in the first register; the executor operates in the second.

Three search algorithms handle different problem classes. GBFS with goal-directed heuristics handles problems with deterministic plans. Enforced hill-climbing handles problems where the heuristic landscape is smooth enough to avoid plateaus. AO* constructs full AND-OR trees for problems requiring conditional branching plans — where the correct next action depends on the outcome of an observation that has not yet been made. The heuristics — world count, unsatisfied goal conjuncts, and epistemic distance — differ in how they measure proximity to \(\varphi_g\) in the space of epistemic states. Their relative advantage is domain-dependent and grows with problem complexity in ways that remain an active area of investigation within the project.
